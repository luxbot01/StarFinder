    About StarFinder

    How dark is the sky where you want to sleep?

    StarFinder maps every Texas state park, state natural area and state historic site, every National Park Service unit in Texas, and the national forests and grasslands, and gives each a star rating for how much of the night sky you can expect to see there. It is free, it has no ads and it does not track you.

    What the stars mean

    The rating comes from the share of the stars a pristine sky shows that should still be visible to the naked eye at the place. Twenty percentage points per star, in half stars: five stars means 90% or more; one star means about a fifth. A city centre keeps only a few percent.

    Behind it is the modelled brightness of the sky straight overhead, in magnitudes per square arcsecond (a natural sky is about 22.0; a city centre about 17 to 18). From that comes the faintest star you could see, and from that, how many stars. These are estimates from a model, not measurements; moonlight, weather, haze, smoke, your eyes and how long you let them adapt all change what you will see.

    How the sky glow is made

    We took NASA's Black Marble nightly satellite radiance (VIIRS Day/Night Band, gap-filled and corrected for moonlight and angle) for twelve nights from August to October 2026, kept the median of each half-kilometre pixel so that clouds and one-off fires drop out, and spread every lit pixel's light across the sky around it with the standard distance law for sky glow (brightness falling with distance to the power 2.5, plus atmospheric loss beyond about 100 km, out to 350 km). Two places with a published Bortle class 3 (Enchanted Rock and South Llano River) set the scale. It is our own model, in the family of the world light-pollution atlases; it is not their data.

    Known limits: the satellite cannot see much of the blue in white LED light, so newer LED-lit places may be brighter than shown; very bright city cores saturate the satellite product at 38 nW/cm²/sr; and the Bortle scale is a whole-sky judgement by eye, while this is the zenith only.

    The forecast

    Opening a place asks the National Weather Service for its forecast at that park's coordinates (if it does not answer, Open-Meteo is asked instead). Each day shows the high, that night's low, the highest chance of rain or snow in the day, and the expected amount. The icon comes from the forecast's own weather types and sky cover; hail shows only when the forecast itself names hail. "Clearest night" averages the forecast cloud cover from 9 PM to 3 AM, and the moon figure is worked out from the average lunar month, good to a few percent. Forecasts are kept in your browser for 30 minutes.

    Before you go

    Check the park's own page for closures, burn bans and reservations, and pick a night near the new moon. StarFinder keeps nothing about you; "Nearest first" uses your location only inside your browser, and the forecast sends only the park's coordinates.
